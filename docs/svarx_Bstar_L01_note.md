# Model B*(L0-1): SVAR-X matrices with GSCPI at lags 0 and 1 (variant)

Generated by `python scripts/run_svarx_matrices.py l01`; workbook in `outputs/svarx/svarx_Bstar_L01_matrices.xlsx`. **This is a labelled variant, not production B\*.** Only the shortage block of the CPI equation differs from B*: $s_t$ and $s_{t-1}$ (the GSCPI) enter as two separate regressors (the `gscpi2` specification of the validation work) instead of their two-quarter mean. Data, sample (1990Q1–2026Q2, $N=146$, $V/U$ for 2025Q4 bridged), restrictions, and the wage and both expectations equations are identical to B*. HAC standard errors (Newey–West, 4 lags, restriction-adjusted); classical standard errors in the workbook. Rows and columns of every matrix follow the order of $\mathbf{y}_t$ and $\mathbf{x}_t$ given in section 1.

## 1. Layout and notation

The structural SVAR-X is

$$
A_0\,\mathbf{y}_t=\mathbf{c}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}A_i\,\mathbf{y}_{t-i}+\sum_{j=0}^{4}B_j\,\mathbf{x}_{t-j}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t
$$

with endogenous vector $\mathbf{y}_t$ (wages, prices, long-run and short-run expectations) and exogenous vector $\mathbf{x}_t$:

$$
\mathbf{y}_t=\begin{bmatrix}
g^{w}_{t} \\
\pi_{t} \\
\pi^{e,L}_{t} \\
\pi^{e,S}_{t}
\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{x}_t=\begin{bmatrix}
\bar{g}^{\,\mathrm{pty}}_{t} \\
(V/U)_{t} \\
\mathrm{cu}_{t} \\
g^{\mathrm{rpe}}_{t} \\
g^{\mathrm{rpf}}_{t} \\
s_{t} \\
D^{\mathrm{20Q2}}_{t} \\
D^{\mathrm{20Q3}}_{t}
\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{c}=\begin{bmatrix}
c_{g^{w}} \\
c_{\pi} \\
0 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$A_0$ holds the contemporaneous links among the endogenous variables, $A_1,\dots,A_4$ their lags, $B_0,\dots,B_4$ the current and lagged exogenous drivers, and $\mathbf{c}$ the constants (only the wage and CPI equations have one). Each equation is estimated one at a time by restricted least squares, so the representation is a re-expression of four regressions, not a jointly estimated system.

| Code name | Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `gw` | $g^{w}_t$ | wage growth (ECI wages and salaries) |
| `gcpi` | $\pi_t$ | CPI inflation |
| `cf10` | $\pi^{e,L}_t$ | long-run expected inflation (Cleveland Fed, 10-year) |
| `cf1` | $\pi^{e,S}_t$ | short-run expected inflation (Cleveland Fed, 1-year) |
| `magpty` | $\bar{g}^{\,\mathrm{pty}}_t$ | trend productivity growth (8-quarter moving average) |
| `vu` | $(V/U)_t$ | job openings per unemployed worker |
| `diffcpicf` | $\mathrm{cu}_t$ | wage catch-up term |
| `grpe`, `grpf` | $g^{\mathrm{rpe}}_t$, $g^{\mathrm{rpf}}_t$ | growth of the relative price of energy, of food |
| `shortage` | $s_t$ | GSCPI (quarterly mean) |
| `D2020Q2`, `D2020Q3` | $D^{\mathrm{20Q2}}_t$, $D^{\mathrm{20Q3}}_t$ | wage-equation dummies for 2020Q2 and 2020Q3 |

The wage equation has no contemporaneous endogenous term (wages respond to everything with a one-quarter lag). $A_0$ is therefore lower triangular with a unit diagonal: CPI responds to current wage growth, long-run expectations to current CPI, and short-run expectations to current CPI and current long-run expectations. A zero is a structural restriction.

### The vector $\mathbf{x}_{t-j}$

$\mathbf{x}_t$ stacks the exogenous drivers and deterministic terms. The dummies are contemporaneous ($B_0$) only. Two points.

(i) The catch-up term is a constructed regressor,

$$
\mathrm{cu}_t=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3}\pi_{t-k}-\pi^{e,S}_{t-4},
$$

i.e. a linear combination of past CPI inflation and past short-run expectations. It is kept in $\mathbf{x}_t$ (as the authors do) rather than folded into $A_i$, which would require lags up to 8; its coefficients are small and insignificant.

(ii) $s_t$ is the GSCPI (quarterly mean). It enters at lag 0 (in $B_0$) and lag 1 (in $B_1$) with free coefficients, so the timing is directly readable: the $B_0$ entry is the same-quarter effect, the $B_1$ entry the one-quarter-later effect.

Individual lag coefficients are noisy and often offsetting (for example $\pi^{e,L}$ in the short-run expectations equation: $1.35$ at lag 0, then $-0.63$, $0.08$, $0.15$, $-0.41$); read the sums. In each equation the coefficients on the variables in the restricted blocks sum to one (homogeneity):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{wage:}\quad & \sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{11}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{14}=1\\
\text{CPI:}\quad & \sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{22}+a_{21}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{21}=1\\
\text{long-run exp.:}\quad & \sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{33}+a_{32}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{32}=1\\
\text{short-run exp.:}\quad & \sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{44}+a_{43}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{43}+a_{42}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{42}=1
\end{aligned}
$$

so the two sums in each pair share an identical standard error by construction.

## 7. Units: yoy or qoq?

**Quarter-on-quarter, annualised** ($400\times$ the change in the log), not year-on-year. Defined in `bb_inflation/data/transforms.py`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g^{w}_t&=400\,\Delta\ln \mathrm{ECI}_t, & \pi_t&=400\,\Delta\ln \mathrm{CPI}_t,\\
g^{\mathrm{rpe}}_t&=400\,\Delta\ln\!\big(\mathrm{CPI}^{\mathrm{energy}}_t/\mathrm{ECI}_t\big), & g^{\mathrm{rpf}}_t&=400\,\Delta\ln\!\big(\mathrm{CPI}^{\mathrm{food}}_t/\mathrm{ECI}_t\big),\\
\bar{g}^{\,\mathrm{pty}}_t&=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{k=0}^{7}400\,\Delta\ln \mathrm{PTY}_{t-k}, & \mathrm{cu}_t&=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3}\pi_{t-k}-\pi^{e,S}_{t-4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

| Variable | Construction | Unit in the regression |
|---|---|---|
| $g^{w}_t$ | ECI wages and salaries, private industry, SA | % qoq annualised |
| $\pi_t$ | CPI-U all items, SA, value of the quarter's last month (not the quarterly average) | % qoq annualised (a 3-month change) |
| $\pi^{e,S}_t$, $\pi^{e,L}_t$ | Cleveland Fed 1-year and 10-year expected inflation, last month of quarter | **level**, % per year (already an annual-rate concept) |
| $g^{\mathrm{rpe}}_t$, $g^{\mathrm{rpf}}_t$ | CPI energy or food relative to the ECI | % qoq annualised, relative to wages |
| $\bar{g}^{\,\mathrm{pty}}_t$ | 8-quarter moving average of productivity growth | % annualised |
| $(V/U)_t$ | JOLTS openings over unemployed | ratio (level) |
| $s_t$ | GSCPI quarterly mean | level (standard deviations) |
| $\mathrm{cu}_t$ | 4-quarter average of $\pi$ minus $\pi^{e,S}$ four quarters earlier | percentage points (a yoy-type gap) |

So the wage and CPI equations are in qoq annualised terms, and the two expectations equations are in levels of an annual rate. Year-on-year figures in the Phase 4 reports are derived afterwards (four-quarter averages of the simulated qoq rates), not estimated. The four lags therefore span one year of quarterly growth rates.

## 8. How inflation expectations enter the wage and CPI equations

**Wage equation: directly, through short-run expectations only.** Writing $\phi_i=[A_i]_{11}$ and $\kappa_i=[A_i]_{14}$,

$$
g^{w}_t=\cdots+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\phi_i\,g^{w}_{t-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\kappa_i\,\pi^{e,S}_{t-i}+\cdots,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{4}\phi_i+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\kappa_i=1
$$

Lagged wages sum to $0.540$ (s.e. $0.142$) and lagged short-run expectations to $0.460$. This is the homogeneity restriction: a sustained $1$ pp rise in $\pi^{e,S}$ raises wage growth by $1$ pp in the long run (a vertical long-run Phillips curve). The individual weights are $\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_4 = 0.197, 0.059, 0.045, 0.159$. Long-run expectations $\pi^{e,L}$ do not enter the wage equation; they reach wages only through $\pi^{e,S}$. A second, weaker route is the catch-up term, which contains $\pi^{e,S}_{t-4}$ (sum of its coefficients $-0.002$, $p=0.96$: nil).

**CPI equation: no direct expectations term; only indirectly through wages.**

$$
\pi_t=a_{21}\,g^{w}_t+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{21}\,g^{w}_{t-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}[A_i]_{22}\,\pi_{t-i}+\cdots
$$

Price inflation depends on four lags of CPI ($0.142$) and current plus four lags of wage growth ($0.858$), summing to one. Expectations affect prices only because they raise wages: $\pi^{e,S}_{t-1}\to g^{w}_t$ with a one-quarter lag, then $g^{w}_t\to\pi_t$ through $a_{21}=0.456$ in the same quarter. First-round effect of $+1$ pp in $\pi^{e,S}$: wage growth $+0.197$ pp the next quarter and CPI $+0.090$ pp in that same quarter (annualised qoq pp).

**The loop.** Expectations respond to inflation, with the first-round chain

$$
\pi_t\;\longrightarrow\;\pi^{e,S}_t\;\longrightarrow\;g^{w}_{t+1}\;\longrightarrow\;\pi_{t+1},\qquad \pi_t\;\longrightarrow\;\pi^{e,L}_t\;\longrightarrow\;\pi^{e,S}_t
$$

Short-run expectations respond by $0.136$ (sum over current and 4 lags, HAC s.e. $0.028$) and long-run expectations by $0.028$ (s.e. $0.008$) per pp of inflation, with strong persistence (own-lag sum of $\pi^{e,L}$: $0.972$). Because the responses are small and slow, price shocks fade quickly while a sustained rise in inflation drifts expectations (and hence the steady state) up. The paper reads this as expectations being not de-anchored; the weak catch-up term is the other half of why there is little wage-price spiral.

## 9. Blanchard–Bernanke (2023): key findings

*What Caused the US Pandemic-Era Inflation?* NBER WP 31417 (June 2023), later AEJ: Macroeconomics 2025. Summarised from the working-paper text.

**Approach**
- Four-equation model of wages, prices, short-run and long-run inflation expectations, estimated quarterly from 1990Q1 as a hybrid that approximates an SVAR with exogenous variables: four lags of everything, restrictions only on contemporaneous links, homogeneity (coefficients sum to one) in each equation.
- Exogenous drivers: $V/U$ (labour-market tightness), relative energy and food prices, a shortage index (Google searches for "shortage"), trend productivity.
- Wage and expectations equations estimated pre-COVID (to 2019Q4), price equation on the full sample so the shortage variable has variation; the pre-COVID relations are then tested on the COVID period.

**Main results**
- Most of the 2021 surge came from shocks to prices given wages (energy, food, sectoral shortages), not from an overheated labour market. Energy explains much of the rise in late 2021 and H1 2022 and the fall in H2 2022; shortages dominated in 2021Q2 and stayed significant.
- Tight labour markets contributed little early on (negative in 2020 to early 2021). Their contribution grows over time but was still a minority share in early 2023.
- The wage Phillips curve with $V/U$ worked in a linear form, wages in 2021–22 were broadly in line with pre-COVID relations, and $V/U$ is a better tightness measure than the unemployment rate in this episode.
- Little wage-price spiral: no evidence of a catch-up effect (workers did not recoup unexpected inflation), and expectations did not de-anchor. Short-run expectations react modestly to inflation (sum $\approx 0.12$–$0.13$ pre-COVID and full sample), long-run barely ($\approx 0.03$).
- Price-shock effects on inflation are short-lived (mostly gone the quarter after, a small permanent effect of $\approx 0.1$ pp for energy), unlike the 1970s. Energy and food long-run pass-through is somewhat larger than their CPI weights (second-round price-price effects).
- Labour-market overheating is more persistent: a sustained one-standard-deviation rise in $V/U$ ($0.5$) raises inflation $\approx 0.7$ pp after 6 quarters, $\approx 1.0$ pp after 12, then about $0.15$ pp a year, because inflation in this model has no fixed steady state and expectations drift with realised inflation.
- Price shocks still explained more of the excess of wage growth over initial conditions than $V/U$ did in early 2023.

**Outlook and policy**
- As commodity and shortage effects fade, the labour-market share rises, so disinflation needs $V/U$ to come down. Conditional projections from 2023Q2 (energy and food flat in relative terms, shortage normal): $V/U$ held near $1.8$ gives no decline; $V/U$ to $1.2$ over 8 quarters gives CPI inflation $\approx 2.7\%$; $V/U$ to $0.8$ gives $\approx 2\%$.
- The unemployment cost depends on the Beveridge curve: if matching efficiency normalises, limited rise in unemployment; if not, the natural rate consistent with $V/U=1.2$ is $\approx 4.3\%$ rather than $3.6\%$.
- Lesson: a simple, traditional wage-price model explains the episode; policy should watch product markets as well as labour markets, and labour-market balance remains the central concern for price stability.

**How B\* differs from the paper (from this project's replication):** GSCPI replaces Google Trends, the sample runs to 2026Q2 and all four equations use it, and the price-equation wage weight has risen from $\approx 0.69$ to $\approx 0.84$ (Phase 4c) while the catch-up term stays zero.
